# Software fit and external validation: how practitioners decide a pain is buildable and worth building

Scope note for the writer: this covers (a) tests that separate software-product-shaped pains from gripes, platform complaints, advice-seeking and adjacent pains, and (b) external demand validation (competitors, review mining, search data, job posts, spend, pre-sales). Evidence strength is tagged inline: **[measured]** = a documented number or case; **[opinion]** = practitioner advice without data; **[vendor]** = published by a company selling a tool for the step it describes. Dates are as shown on the page; "undated" means the page shows none. Research date: 2026-11-10.

## Key question 1: Tests for software fit (workflow, spreadsheet, "already pay for a worse version", "a human is hired", search intent, measurable JTBD outcome)

### Takeaway
No practitioner source phrases a "workflow test" or "spreadsheet test" in those words; the closest primary statements are April Dunford's "what would a customer do if your offering didn't exist" (answers: spreadsheet, manual process, intern, nothing), Jason Cohen's "naturally recurring pain" versus "point-in-time pain", and the Mom Test's "how do you currently address the problem / how much does it cost you / what alternatives have you investigated". Search-intent and job-post tests exist mostly as vendor guidance, with the consistent caveat that low absolute volume is normal for micro-SaaS and that a job post proves someone pays a person, not that they will pay for software.

### Cited Findings

**Status quo / spreadsheet / manual process as the real competitor**
- April Dunford (Dec 28, 2020): "What would a customer do if your offering didn't exist?" Sometimes the answer is "Do nothing", meaning the customer keeps "a spreadsheet, a manual process, or hiring an intern"; common status-quo solutions are "spreadsheets, documents, manual processes, and the limited features of an existing 'monolith' platform", chosen because they are "easy to start, cheap or free, and often 'good enough in a pinch'". In enterprise software companies "typically lose 20% to 30% of deals to 'no decision'". **[opinion, with the 20-30% figure unsourced on the page]** — [Positioning and Competition](https://aprildunford.substack.com/p/positioning-and-competition)
- Dinuka Nilupul, dev.to (posted "May 12", year not shown; likely 2025): "Workarounds count as competitors. Maybe the most dangerous competitors." "We use a Google Sheet and it's fine" is "harder to beat than a dedicated competitor with a worse UI". He lists how people cope today: spreadsheets, other tools, Notion templates, "outsourcing to an assistant, or ignoring the problem". His own rejected idea (Gaplix) failed because "the workaround tax was too low for anyone to switch tools". **[opinion]** — [How to validate a SaaS idea before you write any code](https://dev.to/dinukanilupul/how-to-validate-a-saas-idea-before-you-write-any-code-92)
- Same post, severity scoring: rate frequency and pain 1-5 and multiply; "Below 9 is probably not worth building for. Above 15 has commercial potential"; the math is "rough on purpose". A daily problem costing ~30 minutes is "real"; a rare mildly annoying problem is "a vitamin, not a painkiller". "If the existing alternatives are genuinely terrible and people are still using them anyway, that's a signal" (he notes it can mean the pain isn't severe enough, or the founder doesn't understand the audience). In "what do you use for X" threads, "Conflicting answers mean nobody has solved it cleanly." **[opinion]** — same URL.
- Rob Fitzpatrick, *The Mom Test* (via Michael Lynch's book report, undated): "Do ask how they currently address the problem your product solves." "Do ask what alternative solutions they've investigated." "Do ask how much problem X currently costs them." "If they haven't looked for ways of solving it already, they're not going to look for (or buy) yours." "Ask about specifics in the past instead of generics or opinions about the future." **[opinion, widely adopted]** — [The Mom Test notes](https://mtlynch.io/book-reports/the-mom-test/)
- Andrew Askins (page shows "a year ago", images dated May 2025): his go/no-go criteria from interviews are "fit with an existing, tedious workflow; current tool spend and willingness to pay; how they discover and trial tools; potential for an ongoing relationship; and trust in AI". He would now ask prospects to "walk me through the last time they bought a new tool (and actually started using it)". **[opinion, from one founder's documented process]** — [My updated idea validation process](https://www.andrewaskins.com/this-time-feels-different-my-updated-idea-validation-process/)

**Recurring pain vs one-off pain; B2B shape (Jason Cohen, MicroConf 2013)**
- Jason Cohen, *Designing the Ideal Bootstrapped Business* (MicroConf 2013; notes by Michael Lynch, Mar 26, 2023): sell "only to businesses (B2B), never consumers, who are too price-sensitive". Good markets: "Naturally recurring pain, such as hosting, invoicing, compliance, or changing digital marketing"; "Products that are not real-time, so a few hours of downtime isn't a disaster"; "Products that can be 'finished' and avoid feature wars"; "Aftermarkets, meaning add-ons or integrations for established products"; "Large markets with room for many niches". Bad markets: "Point-in-time pain, such as weddings or events"; "Viral products"; "Marketplaces, which require attracting two sides at once". Before building, have "20-30 people ready to pay monthly": "If you can't find 20-30 in the first few months, it's going to be hard to reach a sustainable customer base ever." **[opinion from a documented operator; the 20-30 rule is a heuristic, not data]** — [mtlynch notes on Cohen's talk](https://mtlynch.io/notes/designing-the-ideal-bootstrapped-business/)

**"They already pay for a worse version" / price anchored to a business cost (Patrick McKenzie)**
- Patrick McKenzie, TwilioCon 2012 talk (posted May 23, 2013): "You are not taking your customer's money. You are taking the business's money." "on business scales, $100 a month, it doesn't move the needle for most businesses." "Price based on customer perceived value. Don't price based on cost." Pricing anchors he lists: "The fully loaded cost of one employee is a good anchor"; "Truck rolls are an incredibly good anchor for pricing, if you can decrease them"; "Lost business, missing revenues, et cetera, also things that you can sell against." Appointment Reminder's pricing page headline: "Pays for itself in one saved appointment"; tiers $29/$79/$199 matched to appointment values by industry; an HVAC customer: "I charge him about $200 a month, and he tells me he would pay 10 times as much." **[opinion plus one documented case]** — [Selling Your Software To Businesses](https://kalzumeus.com/2013/05/23/selling-your-software-to-businesses-twiliocon-2012-presentation)
- Arvid Kahl, *The Embedded Entrepreneur* ePDF: "you know that there are real people out there feeling a painful problem, and you can check if they are already spending" (extraction is letter-spaced; wording approximate). **[opinion]** — [ePDF](https://assets.super.so/0cc87f49-a510-4536-9ecf-43473b2b7bdd/files/85515890-9037-446c-9d4f-f49c11ea5f84/_The_Embedded_Entrepreneur_ePDF_(web).pdf); a Passionfroot interview page quotes him asking which problems "are critical enough so they have a budget to maybe pay you for having them solved" and "what existing solutions do they already use?" — [Passionfroot](https://www.passionfroot.me/creators-on-air/arvid-kahl)

**"A human is hired to do this" (job posts / VA / freelancer listings)**
- BigIdeasDB (2026, **[vendor]**): the strongest Upwork-derived SaaS opportunities are where "the task is repetitive (done monthly or weekly), clients pay significant amounts ($500+), and the deliverable is relatively standardized"; its validation method is "Search Upwork for that task and count the posts. Volume and recurrence, not any single budget, is your signal." It reports analyzing 5,351 posts across 482 categories, all from its internal platform. — [Validate SaaS demand with Upwork jobs](https://bigideasdb.com/validate-saas-demand-with-upwork-jobs); [How to use Upwork analysis](https://bigideasdb.com/help/how-to-use-upwork-analysis); [State of Freelance Demand 2026](https://bigideasdb.com/state-of-freelance-demand-2026)
- Apify "Upwork MCP" actor (**[vendor]**): "Feed a batch of 500–1,000 raw job postings into your favorite agent and ask it to cluster them by problem type". — [Apify Upwork scraper](https://apify.com/epicscrapers/upwork-scraper-mcp)
- I found no independent (non-vendor) source documenting a conversion from "Upwork posts exist" to "software sold". See Gaps.

**Search-intent tests (Keywords Everywhere, Ahrefs, "alternative to", "X software")**
- Ranktracker SaaS keyword guide (**[vendor]**): "Searches like '[Competitor] alternative' or '[Competitor] vs [Competitor]' tend to have lower search volume but extremely high purchase intent". — [SaaS keyword research guide](https://www.ranktracker.com/blog/saas-keyword-research-guide/)
- dev.to "Why high search volume is a trap for SaaS builders" groups modifiers: "Commercial Intent: 'pricing', 'alternative to', 'vs', 'cost'" and treats "software"/"tool" as transactional. **[opinion]** — [dev.to post](https://dev.to/ideacrystal/why-high-search-volume-is-a-trap-for-saas-builders-2b9)
- Stormy.ai (**[vendor]**): Keywords Everywhere "might show a search volume of only a few hundred per month" for a niche query and argues profitable micro-SaaS niches "often exist in these low-volume, high-intent keywords"; pair volume with Reddit because "search volume tells you that people are looking; Reddit tells you why they are frustrated". — [SaaS keyword research to validate ideas](https://stormy.ai/blog/saas-keyword-research-validate-startup-ideas)
- Grow and Convert (agency, **[measured, small]**): for a set of SaaS articles "Ahrefs showed 0-20 searches per month for each of these articles, we saw significantly higher organic search volume", i.e. tool estimates under-report niche B2B queries. — [SaaS keyword research](https://www.growandconvert.com/content-marketing/saas-keyword-research/)
- Exploding Topics methodology page (undated, **[vendor]**): data sources listed are "Social media platforms", "Search engines", "Online forums and communities", "News articles and blogs", "E-commerce websites", "Podcasts"; models "Analyze historical data to identify growth patterns" and "Detect sudden spikes in interest"; analysts "Reviews the top-scoring trends", "Validates the authenticity and relevance", "Filters out fads (movies, TV shows, celebrity gossip etc.)". The methodology page does not state the "compounding growth over several months" rule; that claim appears only on its blog ("qualified for the database based on its historical Google Search volume growth"). — [Methodology](https://explodingtopics.com/methodology); [How to use ET](https://explodingtopics.com/blog/how-to-use-et)

**Measurable outcome (JTBD / Outcome-Driven Innovation)**
- Ulwick/Strategyn outcome-statement examples: "minimize the time it takes to get the songs in the desired order for listening"; "Minimize the time it takes to identify the correct drill bit size for the material being drilled." Third-party guides give the template "[Direction of improvement] + [Unit of measure] + [Object of control] + [Optional clarifier]" and say the verb "is almost always minimize or maximize"; "Add a Gantt chart" is a solution, "Minimize the time it takes to identify which tasks are behind schedule" is an outcome. **[method, secondary wording]** — [Strategyn slide deck](https://www.slideshare.net/slideshow/turn-jobs-to-be-done-theory-into-practice/40867606); [AIM Institute: creating outcome statements](https://support.theaiminstitute.com/2.-creating-outcome-statements); [koji.so ODI summary](https://www.koji.so/docs/outcome-driven-innovation-odi)

**Pain-signal taxonomy used in community text (Sales Safari)**
- Stacking the Bricks, EP42 "What is Sales Safari?": notes are sorted into "pain, jargon, worldview, and recommendations"; pain shows as "a wide range of negative emotions, which could be things including struggle, confusion, disagreement, debate"; "a question is a great first indicator of pain in your audience"; "Sales Safari is *not* customer interviews, not cold-calling, not surveys, or validation"; it works better B2B because "consumer audiences are fickle and really inconsistent". **[method description, from its authors]** — [EP42](https://stackingthebricks.com/podcast/ep42-what-is-sales-safari-with-eteinne-garbugli/)
- Amy Hoy, "Sales Safari in Action" (Jun 12, 2013): the newer 30x500 teaches to "identify the first level of pain (obvious)" and "identify the second level of pain (why is this even being talked about? what's the core here? why do they care?)". — [Video page](https://stackingthebricks.com/video-sales-safari-in-action/)

**Scalability / shape (YC)**
- Jared Friedman, YC Startup School (Nov 17, 2022): evaluation question on scalability: "Pure software scales easily. Service businesses that depend on skilled labor are riskier." On acuteness: ask "how acute is this problem"; the strongest case is where "the alternative is literally nothing". — [Transcript](https://rosetta.to/u/ycombinator/how-to-get-and-evaluate-startup-ideas-startup-school)
- VarenyaZ (agency, **[vendor]**) process-mapping prompts for a workflow-shaped idea: "Trigger: What event starts the workflow (e.g., new order, compliance change)?", "People and roles", "Tools" (manual methods in use). — [How to validate before building software](https://varenyaz.com/guides/how-to-validate-a-startup-idea-before-building-software-for-modern-businesses/)

### Inferences
- The practitioner consensus assembles into a de-facto software-fit checklist even though nobody publishes it under one name: (1) the pain recurs on a schedule (Cohen), (2) the person already has a workaround with a cost (Dunford, Mom Test, Nilupul), (3) that cost can be anchored to a business number (McKenzie), (4) the outcome can be written as "minimize/maximize [metric] of [object]" (Ulwick), (5) someone already spends money or time on it (Kahl, Upwork guides). A complaint that fails (1), (2) and (5) is a gripe, not a product.
- For an LLM "is this software-solvable" prompt, the sources suggest asking for evidence fields rather than a yes/no: named workaround, named cost, recurrence, outcome statement, and whether the text shows past attempts to solve it (the Mom Test line "if they haven't looked ... they're not going to buy yours" is the sharpest single discriminator against advice-seeking and venting).
- Search-volume thresholds are unreliable for niche B2B (Grow and Convert's 0-20 estimate vs real traffic); the practitioner use is intent classification ("alternative to", "vs", "pricing", "tool") rather than a volume floor.

### Gaps
- I found no source that uses the labels "workflow test" or "spreadsheet test"; those appear to be the pipeline's own names. The closest attributable formulations are quoted above.
- No independent, measured evidence that job-post frequency (Upwork/VA listings) predicts software purchase; only vendor method pages. A job post proves spend on a person, not a subscription (my inference).
- Keywords Everywhere's and Ahrefs' own methodology pages were not fetched; the search-intent claims come from third-party guides.
- Exploding Topics' "compounding growth over several months" rule is stated on its blog, not on its methodology page; treat as marketing.

## Key question 2: Tarpit ideas, YC Requests for Startups, and the shape of pains a small team can build

### Takeaway
YC's tarpit list (consumer ideas, "app to discover new things", "ways to bet on things", rebuilding everything in Web3, friend-coordination/event planning) is defined by a pattern, not a category: ideas that get instant positive feedback, have been tried many times, and where the founder cannot name what changed. YC's current RFS (Winter 2027) and the bootstrapper canon (Cohen, McKenzie, Friedman) point the other way: B2B, recurring, compliance- and paperwork-shaped, vertical, integration/"glue" software, and "boring" spaces with existing competitors.

### Cited Findings

**Tarpit definition and examples**
- Matt Rickard (Dec 11, 2022), summarizing the YC video by Dalton Caldwell and Michael Seibel: tarpit ideas pass basic checks ("they seem interesting and buildable, users say they want them, and they have emotional appeal"). YC's examples: "Consumer ideas", "App to discover new things", "Ways to bet on things", "Rebuilding everything in Web3". Reasons: consumer ideas are "Very reliant on timing" with a very high bar; "Discovery problems are usually not real" because either supply doesn't exist (restaurant choices are finite) or demand doesn't (people prefer popular options). "It's not that these ideas are inherently wrong or bad", but they "require an extra layer of thinking for the questions: why now? why this team?" **[opinion, secondary summary of YC's talk]** — [Tarpit Ideas](https://cdn.matt-rickard.com/tarpit-ideas)
- YC, "Tarpit Ideas: The Sequel" (Jul 19, 2024; auto-captioned transcript): Dalton: "certain idea spaces that seem to always attract a lot of applicants and they never work" (00:01:18). Flavours: "world should work differently" (friend-coordination/event planning: "it's just hard to change human behavior", 00:06:25) and "get rich quickly" (arbitrage, WallStreetBets-inspired, managing friends' money). "Co-pilot for X" is explicitly not a tarpit ("not enough people have tried it yet"). Tests: talk to users and get a customer; explain why new technology "might make something accomplishable that wasn't accomplishable before" (00:04:27); a red flag is ignoring that many others tried and failed; Michael: if you can't "draw any connection between the two technology and why your problem can be solved today" and it has been tried many times, that's tricky; "If it looks too good to be true like if you're getting too much positive feedback ... maybe you should be a little skeptical" (00:12:27). Former tarpits can become viable after a technology shift (YC funded an AI-generated personalized podcast company Dalton "would not have funded before LLMs"). **[opinion from YC partners]** — [Transcript](https://rosetta.to/u/ycombinator/tarpit-ideas-the-sequel)
- Lenny's Newsletter interview with Caldwell (Apr 18, 2024): tarpits are "ideas that receive initial positive feedback but may lead to long-term challenges" and "difficult to pivot away from once their limitations become apparent" (publisher's summary, not verbatim Caldwell). — [Lessons from 1,000+ YC startups](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/lessons-from-1000-yc-startups)
- Jared Friedman, YC Startup School (Nov 17, 2022): tarpits are "popular concepts that look easy but have structural obstacles, like a friend-scheduling app"; advice: "Search for prior attempts first." Four mistakes: a "solution in search of a problem", getting stuck on tarpits, jumping into the first idea, waiting for the perfect idea. Three signs a bad-seeming idea is good: "Hard to get started" (Stripe), "Boring space" (Gusto), "Existing competitors" (Dropbox). Seven recipes include "Look for large industries that seem broken" and "Pick a promising idea space and talk to people in it about their problems"; also "Is the broader category of related ideas known for a good hit rate?" Closing: "the only way to know for sure if your startup idea is good is to Just Launch it and find out". — [Transcript](https://rosetta.to/u/ycombinator/how-to-get-and-evaluate-startup-ideas-startup-school)
- Paul Graham (Nov 2012): "At YC we call these 'made-up' or 'sitcom' startup ideas"; test: "ask yourself: who wants this right now?" and "If you can't answer that, the idea is probably bad." "You can either dig a hole that's broad but shallow, or one that's narrow and deep, like a well"; "Nearly all good startup ideas are of the second type." On schlep blindness (Stripe): founders "shrank from having to deal with payments"; "any sufficiently good idea will have as many" schleps. — [How to Get Startup Ideas](https://paulgraham.com/startupideas.html)

**Positive lists: YC RFS and bootstrapper "good market" shapes**
- YC Requests for Startups, Winter 2027 batch (fetched 2026-11-10): "The $15-an-Hour Expert" (AI letting uncredentialed workers do "tax, benefits, or claims paperwork"), "Plaid for Government Benefits" (consent layer pulling "verified income, tax, and residency records"), "AI for City Hall" ("permits, plan reviews, and inspections, plus 311, public records, and licensing"), "AI for the Air-Gapped Economy" (on-prem AI for defense, banks, hospitals "including security accreditation"), "Own Your Intelligence", "Vertical Foundation Models", plus hardware/space/finance items. No consumer social or discovery/marketplace entries. — [YC RFS](https://www.ycombinator.com/rfs). Lenny's page links older RFS titles "Better enterprise glue" and "New enterprise resource planning software". — [Lenny's](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/lessons-from-1000-yc-startups)
- Jason Cohen's good-market list (hosting, invoicing, compliance, digital marketing; aftermarkets/add-ons; "can be finished"; not real-time) and bad-market list (weddings/events, viral, marketplaces), as cited in Q1. — [mtlynch notes](https://mtlynch.io/notes/designing-the-ideal-bootstrapped-business/)
- Indie Hackers thread "Should I just create a boring B2B SaaS": boring software is "not B2C, often in an unsexy vertical"; "You can build and grow a boring B2B app pretty repeatably since it doesn't rely on the luck you often need with a B2C or highly-competitive space." Same thread on McKenzie's Appointment Reminder: "He had to sell it actively, in-person, to salons, clinics, etc., and even then uptake was poor." **[opinion]** — [IH thread](https://www.indiehackers.com/post/should-i-just-create-a-boring-b2b-saas-b6181991c0)
- dev.to "I checked 100 startup ideas for Reddit demand" (posted Jun 17, year not shown; author is an AI agent promoting its platform, **[weak]**): of 104 ideas, "73 killed as undifferentiated wrappers, 21 with no monetization path, and 10 with verified demand"; survivors fell into "Context Janitor" (data cleaning for LLM ingestion), "Anti-SaaS" (one-time/self-hosted), and "Obscure B2B niche" ("a scheduling tool for dental hygienists that handles insurance billing codes"); advice: "Build boring tools that save people 15 minutes of drudgery." Reddit replies to wrappers: "I can just do this in ChatGPT." Headline arithmetic doesn't reconcile (94/104 ≈ 90%, not 99%). — [dev.to](https://dev.to/howiprompt/i-checked-100-startup-ideas-for-reddit-demand-last-week-heres-the-99-that-dropped-l5i)

**Why LLM+Reddit idea pipelines come out vague (2025-2026 commentary)**
- BigIdeasDB (**[vendor]**, 2026): "The quality of output depends heavily on your prompt engineering — vague prompts produce generic ideas, while specific constraints produce more interesting results"; "neither tool can verify demand." — [Best startup idea generator tools](https://bigideasdb.com/best-startup-idea-generator-tools-2026)
- A Reddit poster (snapshot Mar 16, 2026): "The problem is that we continually hand it dead ideas. Of course the output feels generic. The input was generic." — [Reddit snapshot](https://reddit.sentinel-team.org/posts/1rub1zb/snapshots/2026-03-16T20%3A20%3A55.006238Z)
- Forum Ventures counterpoint: among 1,000+ Reddit-sourced pitches, "the majority of AI pitches are vertical-specific, workflow-embedded, and often framed around compliance or auditability". — [Forum VC](https://www.forumvc.com/thought-pieces/what-1000-startup-ideas-on-reddit-reveal)

### Inferences
- The tarpit test reduces to three questions a pipeline can ask of each theme: (a) has this been attempted repeatedly (search "X app" / Crunchbase / Product Hunt graveyard), (b) what changed since those attempts, (c) is the positive feedback cheap (upvotes, "I'd use that") rather than costly. YC's own tarpit examples are nearly all consumer discovery/coordination/speculation; nothing in the RFS is consumer.
- The RFS and Cohen/McKenzie lists overlap on one shape: paperwork that recurs on a calendar (tax, benefits, claims, permits, compliance, invoicing, appointments). That is the shape most likely to pass Q1's recurrence and cost-anchor tests.
- "Discovery problems are usually not real" is a direct filter for "find me X" / "which tool should I use" posts: those are advice requests, not product pains, unless the asker already pays for a bad answer.

### Gaps
- I could not retrieve the YC library page or a transcript of the original "Avoid these tempting startup ideas" video; the examples come from Matt Rickard's summary and the 2024 sequel.
- No source gives a measured failure rate for tarpit categories; it is YC partners' pattern recognition.

## Key question 3: Competition as validation vs saturation; review mining; competitive alternatives; gap vs "no one wants it"

### Takeaway
PG, Friedman, Dunford and the bootstrapper community treat existing competitors as proof of demand and "alternative to X" as a legitimate wedge; the discriminating question is whether the incumbent's customers are actively complaining in specific terms (gap) or whether prospects choose "do nothing" (no felt pain). I could not find Amy Hoy recommending 1-3 star review mining specifically; Sales Safari is forum/community ethnography that explicitly disclaims being validation. Star-review mining guidance comes from vendors and e-commerce practitioners, who warn that 3-4 star reviews are often richer than 1-star and that 1-star reviews can be manufactured.

### Cited Findings

**Competition as a positive signal**
- Paul Graham (Nov 2012): "Worrying that you're late is one of the signs of a good idea." "It's exceptionally rare for startups to be killed by competitors". "A crowded market is actually a good sign, because it means both that there's demand" and that "none of the existing solutions are good enough." You need "a thesis about what everyone else in it is overlooking"; "Best of all is when you can say that they didn't have the courage of their convictions". **[opinion]** — [paulgraham.com](https://paulgraham.com/startupideas.html)
- Jared Friedman (Nov 17, 2022): "most good startup ideas have competition"; "Entrenched competitors usually require a new insight"; existing competitors are one of three signs a bad-seeming idea is good. — [Transcript](https://rosetta.to/u/ycombinator/how-to-get-and-evaluate-startup-ideas-startup-school)
- Rob Walling, Startups for the Rest of Us (Jul 2025 era episode): his first question to a founder was "Have other companies already tried this idea? And if so, what is Hello Query going to do differently?" — [Episode 676](https://www.startupsfortherestofus.com/episodes/episode-676-exit-valuations-choosing-between-ideas-and-more-listener-questions)
- Discury digest (**[vendor]**): validation means a pain customers "already pay competitors to partly solve"; a market with no competition "typically has no market to capture". — [Discury](https://discury.io/digest/playbook-validating-saas-ideas-without-fear-of-theft-2461)

**Competitive alternatives (April Dunford)**
- Dunford (Dec 28, 2020): think about "Competitive Alternatives" rather than "competitors"; competitors are usually seen as "other solutions that look like ours", while competitive alternatives "start with the status quo" and then include anything prospects consider "when actively hunting for a new solution". Companies that rarely appear on shortlists are "phantom competitors" and should not shape positioning. Understanding alternatives is "the starting point for understanding what differentiates your product in the market"; you must be "clearly superior to the status quo" to get customers to act. — [Positioning and Competition](https://aprildunford.substack.com/p/positioning-and-competition)
- Secondary: a podcast transcript quotes her that the answer to "what would they do if you didn't exist" is "usually a spreadsheet, or a manual process, or just doing nothing" and that "Most companies start with 'who is our customer?' That's the wrong question." **[secondary; wording unverified against the book]** — [stfo.io](https://stfo.io/podcast/april-dunford-product-positioning)

**Saturation vs opportunity (Amy Hoy)**
- Amy Hoy (Aug 4, 2017): calls the "crippling fear of market saturation" misplaced; the total addressable market overstates the real opportunity because only a small share of people are actively looking to buy, and active buyers "are going to find your competition, too"; Sales Safari studies what an audience does online, including what they "complain about, rave about", contrasted with interviews, surveys and validation. — [Competition? SEO? Saturation?!](https://stackingthebricks.com/how-to-survive-and-thrive/)

**Review mining of incumbents (what exists, and who says it)**
- BigIdeasDB "Feature-Gap Method" (**[vendor]**, 2026): read "the three- and four-star reviews of the most-reviewed tools in a category" rather than the 1- and 5-star extremes; G2 lets you sort to 1-2 star reviews; segment by company size (in its example SMB reviewers complained about pricing transparency while mid-market complained about custom reporting limits); five-star reviews show the category's "non-negotiables". — [Turn G2 reviews into SaaS ideas](https://bigideasdb.com/turn-g2-reviews-into-saas-ideas); [G2 review analysis docs](https://bigideasdb.com/docs/g2-review-analysis)
- EvolveAMZ review-mining methodology (Sep 11, 2026, e-commerce **[vendor]**): "Read three-star reviews first" because those buyers kept the product and describe what nearly stopped them; filter 1-star reviews to separate product failures from shipping/wrong-item noise; ~100-150 reviews per competitor before patterns stabilise; Amazon's Conditions of Use prohibit "data mining, robots and similar data gathering and extraction tools". — [EvolveAMZ](https://evolveamz.com/review-mining-competitor-reviews-ecommerce/)
- BuzzFeed News investigation: sellers post "tasks" that include "leaving positive reviews on their products — or one-star reviews on competitors' products". **[measured, journalism]** — [BuzzFeed News](https://www.buzzfeednews.com/article/nicolenguyen/Amazon-fake-review-problem)
- Capterra review integrity is disputed (critical reviewers report generic rejections) and G2 acquired Capterra, Software Advice and GetApp from Gartner in 2026. — [checkthat.ai on Capterra](https://checkthat.ai/brands/capterra/reviews)
- Indie Hackers keyword post: a founder reports "a high volume of traffic coming from YouTube reviews and 'Alternative To' sites" (evidence that alternative-seeking is a real acquisition channel). — [IH post](https://www.indiehackers.com/post/3-tools-i-use-for-saas-keyword-research-1-i-built-962a2ab251)

**Gap vs "no one wants it"**
- Dinuka Nilupul: "If the existing alternatives are genuinely terrible and people are still using them anyway, that's a signal" that can mean either low pain or misunderstanding; his own idea was killed because "the workaround tax was too low for anyone to switch tools"; in community threads "Conflicting answers mean nobody has solved it cleanly." — [dev.to](https://dev.to/dinukanilupul/how-to-validate-a-saas-idea-before-you-write-any-code-92)
- Dunford: 20-30% of enterprise deals go to "no decision"; the status quo wins when it is "good enough in a pinch". — [Positioning and Competition](https://aprildunford.substack.com/p/positioning-and-competition)
- Friedman: the strongest problem is one where "the alternative is literally nothing". — [Transcript](https://rosetta.to/u/ycombinator/how-to-get-and-evaluate-startup-ideas-startup-school)
- Secondary summary of Jason Cohen: "Incumbents ignore markets below their revenue materiality threshold, so a niche too small for them can be exactly big enough for you." **[secondary paraphrase]** — [antoinebuteau.com](https://www.antoinebuteau.com/lessons-from-jason-cohen/)

### Inferences
- A "gap" in review text is credible when the complaint (a) names a specific missing capability or workflow break, (b) recurs across reviewers in the same segment, and (c) the reviewer is still paying (3-4 star, or 1-2 star with "we're stuck with it"). A review that says "too expensive" or "support was slow" is a positioning or ops complaint, not a product gap.
- "No one wants it" shows up as the opposite pattern: incumbents with few reviews overall, prospects describing "do nothing" / a free spreadsheet as fine, and advice threads with a consensus answer. Conflicting answers in "what do you use" threads are the inverse signal (Nilupul).
- For a pipeline whose wedge must quote a real review: require the quote to pass (a)-(c), and prefer 2-4 star reviews over 1-star, which are both noisier and more likely to be manufactured.

### Gaps
- No primary Amy Hoy / 30x500 page recommending 1-3 star review reading was found after two targeted searches; the technique is widely attributed to her informally but the attribution is unverified. If the pipeline cites her for it, it should instead cite Sales Safari's forum-based pain taxonomy.
- April Dunford's book text (Obviously Awesome, 2019) was not fetched; the Substack post is the primary used.
- No measured study comparing outcomes of founders who review-mined vs not; all guidance is method description.

## Key question 4: Pre-build demand validation methods, their cost, and benchmarks (smoke tests, pre-sales, fake door, concierge, waitlist conversion, cold outreach); why community text alone cannot prove willingness to pay

### Takeaway
Documented cases (Buffer 2011, Drip 2014, WP Engine 2010-2013, MetaMonster 2024-25) show the ladder of commitment practitioners use: email signup → clicked a paid plan → "committed to buy" at a stated price → prepaid. Benchmarks for the first rung (landing-page conversion) vary 2-25% by traffic source and are mostly vendor-published; the rungs that matter are pre-payment counts (Cohen: 30 paid pre-launch; Walling: 11 of 17 committed at $99/mo) and the recurring finding that interest without payment converts near zero (Askins: 300-person list, 9 "interested" prospects, none paid).

### Cited Findings

**Documented cases**
- Buffer, Joel Gascoigne (Feb 16, 2011): a two-page MVP was shared on Twitter; "The aim of this two-page MVP was to check whether people would even consider using the app"; then "adding a page in between the two which showed pricing" which "tests the pricing (by detecting which plan they click on)"; result "a small number of people were clicking on paid plans"; "After this result, I didn't hesitate to start building the first minimal version"; first paying customer within 4 days of launch; ~2.5 months after launch 500+ users and ~4% upgrading. No signup counts given for the tests. **[measured case, partial numbers]** — [Buffer](https://buffer.com/resources/idea-to-paying-customers-in-7-weeks-how-we-did-it/)
- Drip, Rob Walling (MicroConf 2014, third-party notes undated): "Rob mailed 17 people" asking "if they would pay 99$/month for it"; "Be very upfront about price"; "11 people were committed to buy"; built a list of 3,200 emails; "Creating the email course is the hardest part so he offered a concierge service"; "Divide your list into cohorts"; "Launch emails sequence with a time-limited discount"; "to 7k/mo in recurring revenues in the first month". No threshold ("10 yeses") is stated on this page. **[measured case, secondary notes]** — [PhraseExpander notes](https://www.phraseexpander.com/microconf-2014/rob-walling-validate-idea-launch7k-microconf-2014/); Mixergy quotes Walling: "I knew 17 people that I thought could feasibly use Drip ... I sent an email saying, 'This is the bulleted list of what I'm looking to build" — [Mixergy](https://mixergy.com/interviews/drip-with-rob-walling/)
- WP Engine, Jason Cohen (via Justin Jackson, Jan 17, 2013): Cohen asked ~50 founders whether they'd pay for enterprise-grade WordPress hosting, ~40 said yes, and "I had 30 actually pay me before launch." Walling's Drip email asked: "I want to know if you would use and pay for it." Jackson's thesis: "Building something people want is not enough"; "You have to be able to market it at a cost less than what the customer will pay you back over time." **[measured case, secondhand]** — [Justin Jackson](https://www.justinjackson.ca/why-its-not-enough-to-build-a-product-that-people-want)
- Cohen's paid-interview recruiting (MicroConf 2013): found 40 WordPress consultants on LinkedIn, offered to pay "whatever [they] thought was fair" to talk; 100% agreed, 38 scheduled, none asked for money. — [mtlynch notes](https://mtlynch.io/notes/designing-the-ideal-bootstrapped-business/)
- MetaMonster, Andrew Askins (2025): mailing list of 300, "a handful signed up, none paid"; 9 prospects said they were interested at stated prices "from $50 to low thousands per month", none paid; alpha of 13 testers, 5 used it meaningfully. Costs: UserInterviews.com "$49 per session, plus $98 for B2B targeting, plus $30–50 incentives, or nearly $200 per session", 5-10 interviews/week realistic; cold email offering "$50–$150 for 30 minutes" (sourced via Apollo.io, sent via Instantly.ai) "was the only offer that worked before launch". Lessons: "people always think they'll be more willing to pay than they actually are"; "I would also try to take pre-payments rather than just asking for a general commitment"; the landing page "isn't about product validation for me anymore" but about testing positioning and channels via Google Ads; "there is no box you can check to say, 'yes this is validated, it will work now.'" He frames this as the 20-hour stage of Walling's "2/20/200" framework. **[measured negative case]** — [Askins](https://www.andrewaskins.com/this-time-feels-different-my-updated-idea-validation-process/)

**Smoke test / fake door / pre-sale / concierge definitions and thresholds**
- Kromatic, Real Startup Book (undated): a landing page smoke test "tests demand, not product quality or whether you can deliver"; measure "CTA completions ÷ unique visitors"; thresholds by source: "Paid search, email signup: 5–15% typical; above 10% is strong"; "Paid social, email signup: 2–5% typical; above 5% is strong"; "Simulated purchase click, paid traffic: 1–3% is a good signal"; "at least 200 unique visitors for a directional read"; set the threshold before launch. Cannot prove "Willingness to pay: an email signup isn't a purchase"; false positives from "warm-audience traffic, curiosity clicks, and urgency tactics". Related: the simulated "buy now" variant "is effectively a fake door test"; a Pre-Sales Test "tests willingness to pay real money"; a Concierge Test means "manually deliver the promised value to early signups". **[method, thresholds unsourced]** — [Kromatic](https://kromatic.com/real-startup-book/2-evaluative-market-experiments/value-proposition-test/landing-page-smoke-test/)
- Dinuka Nilupul: smoke-test target "15 to 20 percent visit-to-email conversion. Below 10 percent suggests the messaging or offer is wrong, or there's no demand. Below 5 percent, walk away"; waitlist-to-paid: "Plan for 10 percent conversion at best"; pre-orders with a small deposit (example $5); "Ten [cold interviews] is the minimum. Five is anecdote"; "Willingness to pay is the only signal that matters." A free signup shows interest, a paid pre-order shows "intent". **[opinion]** — [dev.to](https://dev.to/dinukanilupul/how-to-validate-a-saas-idea-before-you-write-any-code-92)

**Waitlist / landing conversion benchmarks (all vendor-published)**
- Flowjam (2026, **[vendor]**, no dataset shown): 0-2% "broken"; 2-5% "working but underperforming"; 5-10% "healthy" (placed alongside Notion, Tana, "most YC-stage waitlists at launch"); 10-15% "strong" (Beehiiv, Linear); 15-25% "top decile" (Robinhood, Substack); 25%+ "Usually warm traffic", with Superhuman's ~27% on invite-only traffic (attributed to First Round Review) as the "public ceiling"; warm traffic converts "2-3x" cold. — [Flowjam](https://www.flowjam.com/blog/waitlist-conversion-calculator)
- LaunchList glossary (**[vendor]**): "The median waitlist converts about 11% of page visitors into signups". — [LaunchList glossary](https://feedback.getlaunchlist.com/glossary/waitlist-conversion-rate)
- A Medium post cites "2–5%" average with top performers "10–15% or higher"; another vendor table lists 25-45% warm, 10-20% cold, 3-10% paid ads. **[vendor/opinion, unsourced]** — [Medium](https://medium.com/@soyzamudio/maximizing-waitlist-conversion-rates-data-driven-strategies-6856cb91f83b); [SeedProd](https://www.seedprod.com/coming-soon-page-conversion-rate/)
- General landing pages: Unbounce 2024 all-industry median 6.6%, with the caveat "Treat as a benchmark median, not proof your page 'should' convert at 6.6%". — [ClickMinded](https://www.clickminded.com/landing-page-statistics/)
- Statistical caution: with a 5% benchmark, a result "between 2.5% and 7.5%" cannot be distinguished from the benchmark at small samples. — [Wrkshp experiment recipe](https://medium.com/wrkshp/experiment-recipe-landing-page-d51407748e2)

**Cold outreach reply rates (2025 vendor datasets; sales outreach, not founder discovery)**
- Belkins: 7,530,489 cold emails sent in 2025, 34,393 replies, 0.45% average (unique replies ÷ sends, excluding auto-replies); founders/owners replied at 0.57%, C-level 0.42%; 0-10 employee companies 0.72% vs 10,000+ at 0.22%. **[measured, vendor]** — [Belkins](https://belkins.io/blog/cold-email-response-rates)
- Instantly: 3.43% average 2025 reply rate, top quartile 5.5%, top 10% of senders above 10.7%. **[measured, vendor, different denominator]** — [Instantly](https://instantly.ai/blog/cold-email-reply-rate-benchmarks)
- Hunter: 4.5% average sequence reply rate across 31M emails in 2025; 7.4% with open tracking off vs 4.4% on. **[measured, vendor]** — [Hunter](https://hunter.io/the-state-of-cold-email-2025)
- Digital Bloom: founders/CEOs as senders achieved 7.63% average reply rate. **[vendor, method unclear]** — [Digital Bloom](https://thedigitalbloom.com/learn/cold-outbound-reply-rate-benchmarks/)

**Why community text alone cannot validate willingness to pay**
- The Mom Test (via mtlynch): "Compliments are the fool's gold of customer learning: shiny, distracting, and worthless." "People are often overly optimistic about paying for a product when it's hypothetical." Commitment means "giving up something they value such as time, reputation, or money"; "The more they're giving up, the more seriously you can take what they're saying." "The only way to fail is by not asking for any commitment from the customer." Real commitments: a clear next meeting, a non-trivial trial, a letter of intent, a pre-order, a deposit. — [mtlynch](https://mtlynch.io/book-reports/the-mom-test/)
- Stacking the Bricks states Sales Safari "is *not* customer interviews, not cold-calling, not surveys, or validation" — i.e. its own authors do not claim community reading validates demand. — [EP42](https://stackingthebricks.com/podcast/ep42-what-is-sales-safari-with-eteinne-garbugli/)
- GummySearch's validation guide (undated; the product "is closed as of 11/30/2025"): signs of a validated problem include customers who "have tried and failed to solve it", "are willing to pay", "raise the same pain points repeatedly", and "can be reached through reliable channels"; "Market risk (whether anyone will pay) and channel risk (whether you can reach buyers affordably) usually matter more than product risk"; warning: "If interview requests get no traction, the problem may not be real or you may be in the wrong communities." **[vendor, now defunct]** — [GummySearch docs](https://gummysearch.com/docs/how-to-validate-business-problems-your-startup-will-solve-jilf0); [GummySearch homepage closure notice](https://gummysearch.com/)
- Arvid Kahl, Product Hunt AMA: "I advise you to monetize immediately. if they aren't willing to pay, do you really want them on the platform anyway?" — [Product Hunt AMA](https://www.producthunt.com/discussions/i-built-and-sold-a-successful-saas-published-two-books-and-help-founders-help-themselves-ama)

### Inferences
- The measured cases rank validation signals by cost to the prospect: upvote/comment (free) < email signup (2-15% of visitors) < clicked paid plan (Buffer, "a small number") < stated commitment at a named price (Drip 11/17 ≈ 65%; Askins 9/9 then 0 paid) < prepayment (WP Engine 30; Cohen's "20-30 ready to pay monthly"). Only the last two rungs survived contact with launch in the documented cases; the stated-commitment rung produced a 100% false positive in Askins' case.
- Reply-rate benchmarks are for sales email; the only founder-discovery number with a mechanism is Cohen's and Askins' "offer to pay for 30 minutes" tactic (Cohen: 40/40 agreed; Askins: "the only offer that worked"). A pipeline that outputs cold-outreach targets should budget for low single-digit replies unless it pays for time.
- Community text can establish pain language, recurrence, workaround, and segment (Sales Safari's four quadrants); by its own authors' statement it cannot establish willingness to pay. The Mom Test's criterion (did they give up time, reputation or money) is not observable in a Reddit post except indirectly ("we pay $X for Y and it still...", "I hired someone to...").

### Gaps
- No independent, non-vendor dataset of waitlist conversion rates was found; Flowjam, LaunchList, SeedProd and Medium figures are unsourced or self-reported.
- Rob Walling's "Validate your SaaS idea FAST" episode page (MicroConf, Feb 8, year unshown) carries no transcript; the step list could not be quoted.
- No founder-discovery-specific cold-email reply-rate dataset exists in the results; all 2025 figures are sales outreach.
- The classic concierge-MVP case (Food on the Table, in *The Lean Startup*) was not fetched; Kromatic's definition is used instead.

## Key question 5: Market-sizing heuristics for niche software from community size, subreddit subscribers, forum members, job counts; minimum workable sizes

### Takeaway
Jason Cohen's rule (roughly 10M consumers or 100K businesses with the problem) and Andrew Askins' price-indexed minimums ($1M ARR needs ~1.08M market at $20/mo down to ~22K at $1K/mo, assuming 25% reach) are the only explicit thresholds with reasoning; Arvid Kahl uses community presence as a proxy (a Facebook group over 5,000 for a sub-niche suggests a large market; no findable group suggests too small or elsewhere) and says to count deciders, not users. Subreddit subscriber counts are treated by every source as a weak signal versus active posters.

### Cited Findings
- Jason Cohen (Feb 16, 2025): "For B2C, you might need a large pool—potentially around 10 million individuals with the problem your product solves"; for B2B, "100,000 businesses can be enough" citing "higher conversion rates and willingness to spend on solutions"; companion post titled "Plausible: Do 10M people or 100k companies have the problem?" (not fetched). Willingness to pay is learned by asking "what they would pay to resolve them". **[opinion from a documented operator]** — [Who should my target customer be?](https://longform.asmartbear.com/who-should-my-target-customer-be/)
- Jason Cohen (MicroConf 2013): target "$10k/month per founder"; "Aim for about 150 customers"; at 150 customers that implies ~$66/month average price; "Underpricing forces you to find more customers, which is hard." — [mtlynch notes](https://mtlynch.io/notes/designing-the-ideal-bootstrapped-business/)
- Andrew Askins, "Market size heuristics for indie SaaS founders" (undated, data 2023-2024): applies Cohen's "Do 10M people or 100k companies have the problem?"; minimum market for $1M ARR by price point, assuming 25% reach and free-trial conversion benchmarks: "$20/mo: 1.08M; $50/mo: 431,000; $100/mo: 216,000; $250/mo: 87,000; $1k/mo: 22,000; $10k/mo: 3,000"; calls these "absolute minimum" and "on the optimistic side"; freemium would need >4x the visitors. Market counts used: SaaS companies ~72,000 (Vainu) / 34,389 (Crunchbase); software companies ~338,523 (Crunchbase); ad agencies 433,410 global; US real estate brokerages 106,548 firms; "A giant market won't make your next product successful, but a small enough market could kill it"; too large a market "tends to attract VC-backed competition". He defers to Justin Jackson's factors (customers, spend, frequency, willingness to pay). **[reasoned heuristic with cited counts]** — [Askins](https://www.andrewaskins.com/bootstrapped-market-size/)
- Arvid Kahl (Nov 14, 2019): "If there is any number at all, treat it like a ballpark figure"; "count the number of deciders and not just the number of buyers"; "Facebook groups are often a good indicator of size" — a sub-niche group "over five thousand people" suggests a large market; "If you can barely find a group at all, your audience may be too small or just somewhere else"; "Make sure to ask people who work in the industry to lead you to the place where they gather"; B2B methods: industry reports, magazine circulation (ask for ad pricing), podcast hosts, conference vendor maps, search-term competition. **[opinion]** — [Determining the size of your SaaS market](https://thebootstrappedfounder.com/determining-the-size-of-your-saas-market/)
- Reader notes on *The Embedded Entrepreneur*: "Validate: Make sure the audience is interesting for you, is the right size, has exciting problems, and is willing to pay for a solution." **[secondary]** — [Ben Robertson notes](https://benrobertsonis.netlify.app/notes/the-embedded-entrepreneur)
- Reddit-based sizing (all **[vendor]** or anonymous blogs): "Active posters and commenters matter more than subscribers; lively daily threads beat a big dead feed" — [RawNeed](https://rawneed.pages.dev/guides/find-underserved-niches-on-reddit/); "A subreddit with 200k subscribers but low comment depth may produce low-quality leads" and assume "30-50% overlap for closely related communities" when deduplicating — [PainOnSocial](https://painonsocial.com/blog/validate-market-size-on-reddit); a solo micro-SaaS can work on "a few thousand paying businesses" and a $10K-$50K MRR target "typically means 1,000-10,000 potential customers at $20-50/month" — [saasblog](https://saasblog.pages.dev/posts/2025/10/how-developers-use-reddit-to-find-saas-ideas); "2% conversion is realistic for a well-marketed micro SaaS" and "1% conversion from subreddit visitors to email on landing page in niche verticals" — same vendor set, unsourced.
- Subreddit size snapshots (e.g. r/SaaS at 374,943) exist only as undated third-party lists. — [Glama report](https://glama.ai/mcp/servers/@king-of-the-grackles/reddit-mcp-poc/blob/070304756ad6ce7d033fc534078d69afb50834e1/reports/top-50-subreddits-saas-ai-builders.md)

### Inferences
- Combining Cohen and Askins: for a solo bootstrapper targeting ~$10K MRR at $50-100/mo, the floor is on the order of 150-200 paying customers, which with 1-2% capture implies a reachable population of roughly 10,000-20,000 deciders. A subreddit of 20,000 subscribers is therefore borderline unless the posters are deciders (Kahl) and there is evidence of existing spend.
- Subscriber counts should be converted to active-poster counts and deduplicated across overlapping communities (30-50%) before use; none of the sources offer a validated conversion from subscribers to buyers.

### Gaps
- Cohen's companion post "Plausible: Do 10M people or 100k companies have the problem?" was not fetched; the rule is quoted from the Feb 2025 post that links it.
- No source gives a measured relationship between subreddit subscribers (or forum members) and paying customers; all Reddit-sizing numbers are vendor rules of thumb.
- Rob Walling's SaaS Playbook market-size guidance was not retrieved.

## Key question 6: The buyer vs user problem (the complainer is not the budget holder) and how practitioners detect it

### Takeaway
Practitioners detect buyer/user mismatch by asking who signs and who approves (Traction Lab's five-role map; "if your primary contact fills fewer than three ... you're talking to the wrong person"), by counting deciders rather than users (Kahl), and by categorically avoiding segments known not to pay (consumers per Cohen; hobbyists, students and tire-kickers per Indie Hackers posts). No source gives a text-level heuristic for detecting this in community posts; the inferences below are mine.

### Cited Findings
- JDM, The Traction Lab, "Don't sell to users who can't buy" (Jan 12, 2026): founders fall into "optimizing for end-user love"; examples are coaches who love the tool, sleep consultants, department heads making intros, where "none of them can actually sign a contract or approve budget"; proposes an "Economic Buyer Map" of five roles: "users, beneficiaries, payers, approvers, and signers", and "If your primary contact fills fewer than three of these roles, you're probably talking to the wrong person"; warning signs: "A growing number of unpaid pilots" and "glowing testimonials from people who will never, ever pay them"; remedy: get introduced to the buyer and confirm they have the problem and budget, or "redefine your customer as the economic buyer"; "stop collecting feedback from people who can't cut checks." **[opinion]** — [Traction Lab](https://tractionthinking.substack.com/p/dont-sell-to-users-who-cant-buy)
- SyncGTM (2026, **[vendor]**): "Validate every pain point with at least two stakeholders — the end user and the economic buyer"; end users surface daily friction, economic buyers explain why it matters to the business. — [SyncGTM](https://syncgtm.com/blog/how-to-uncover-customer-pain-points-and-needs-in-b2b-sales)
- Patrick McKenzie (May 23, 2013): "You are not taking your customer's money. You are taking the business's money." — [Kalzumeus](https://kalzumeus.com/2013/05/23/selling-your-software-to-businesses-twiliocon-2012-presentation)
- Arvid Kahl (Nov 14, 2019): "count the number of deciders and not just the number of buyers." — [Kahl](https://thebootstrappedfounder.com/determining-the-size-of-your-saas-market/)
- Jason Cohen (2013): never sell to consumers, "who are too price-sensitive". — [mtlynch notes](https://mtlynch.io/notes/designing-the-ideal-bootstrapped-business/)
- Indie Hackers, "The quiet trap that keeps smart founders broke: building for people who don't pay for anything": hobbyists, students and tire-kickers "often never convert"; "if a free Google Sheet is good enough, people won't buy the SaaS"; PricingBot's consumer Chrome extension reached ~3,000 users with few willing to pay while revenue came from e-commerce business owners. **[opinion, anecdote]** — [IH post](https://www.indiehackers.com/post/the-quiet-trap-that-keeps-smart-founders-broke-building-for-people-who-don-t-pay-for-anything-ae8680c72c)
- Flavio Copes, "Selling SaaS to developers": "developers don't pay" can be a misdiagnosis; sometimes the buyer wants to pay but the offer doesn't match how they purchase. **[opinion]** — [flaviocopes.com](https://flaviocopes.com/selling-to-builders/)
- Stacking the Bricks: Sales Safari works better for B2B because "consumer audiences are fickle and really inconsistent". — [EP42](https://stackingthebricks.com/podcast/ep42-what-is-sales-safari-with-eteinne-garbugli/)
- Indie Hackers counterpoints: "consumers are less likely to pay, but markets are bigger and they make impulse buys"; NomadList cited as a working B2C example. — [IH: is B2C SaaS a waste of time](https://www.indiehackers.com/post/is-b2c-saas-a-waste-of-time-c894b3c8eb)

### Inferences
- Text-level cues a pipeline can use to flag "complainer is not the buyer" (my synthesis of the sources' role language, not a published heuristic): first-person role markers ("as a nurse/teacher/employee/intern", "my manager", "our IT won't", "the company uses X"), consumer-role markers ("as a parent/patient/student/hobbyist"), and the absence of any spend verb ("we pay", "I pay", "we switched from", "we hired"). Posts where the author both has the pain and names the budget ("I run a 3-person agency and we pay $X for Y") map to Traction Lab's three-of-five roles.
- The corollary for the brief's "wedge" review quote: an App Store 1-2 star review written by an end user of enterprise software (e.g. a field worker complaining about a scheduling app chosen by their employer) does not evidence a buyable wedge; a review by the owner/admin of a small business does.

### Gaps
- No source provides a validated classifier or wording list for detecting user-vs-buyer status in community text; Traction Lab's map is designed for sales conversations, not scraped posts.
- Lean B2B (Étienne Garbugli) and MEDDIC "economic buyer" primary pages were not fetched; the term's origin in MEDDIC is from a vendor glossary ([dock.us](https://www.dock.us/library/economic-buyer)).
- IdeaBrowser's own page returned HTTP 429; its per-idea metrics (search volume, growth, pain level, feasibility, founder fit, revenue potential, execution pain) are known only from a third-party listing ([stork.ai](https://www.stork.ai/tool/ideabrowser-com)) and a competitor's claim that it "is not a validation tool" ([Preuve](https://preuve.ai/compare/ideabrowser)). GummySearch's post-category taxonomy (pain, solution requests, money talk, advice requests) could not be verified: the product closed 11/30/2025 and the archive could not be fetched.
