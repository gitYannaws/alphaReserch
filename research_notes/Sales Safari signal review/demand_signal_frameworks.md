# Practitioner frameworks for judging whether an observed customer pain is a real, paid, product-worthy demand signal

Research date: 2026-11-10. Tagging convention used below: **[Verbatim]** = the framework's own words, quoted exactly from the cited page; **[Summary]** = my paraphrase of the cited page; **[Secondary]** = a third party's account of the framework (not the author's own text). Where a source was fetched from a mirror or an archive, the mirror URL is given.

The home-built pipeline this is compared against scores forum complaints on intensity, frequency (distinct authors), willingness-to-pay keywords, reachability, recurrence and persistence, then ranks them with a software-solvability multiplier. Each section's Inferences block maps the framework onto those axes.

## Key question 1: Tony Ulwick's Outcome-Driven Innovation opportunity score

### Takeaway
Ulwick's score is additive, not multiplicative: Opportunity = Importance + (Importance − Satisfaction), with the gap floored at zero, so importance is weighted twice and over-serving never lowers a score. Strategyn computes both inputs as top-two-box percentages of survey respondents; the numeric cut-offs for "underserved" (10, 12, 15) circulate only in secondary sources, and Strategyn's own public page defines "underserved" qualitatively.

### Cited Findings
- **[Verbatim]** "Opportunity = Importance + (Importance — Satisfaction)" and "In this algorithm, 'importance' is the percentage of people rating the outcome very or extremely important and 'satisfaction' is the percentage of people rating the outcome very or extremely satisfied." — Eric Eskey, Strategyn, "Quantify Your Customer's Unmet Needs", October 22, 2019 — [Source](https://strategyn.com/quantify-your-customers-unmet-needs)
- **[Verbatim]** "We operationalize this thinking by asking respondents to rate desired outcome statements (which are devoid of solutions) for importance and satisfaction using ordinal, categorical scales. We then compute importance and satisfaction scores based on top-two-box proportions. Consistent with the proper and valid use of statistics on ordinal data, we do not assume the distance between scale points. Nor do we use the scale points themselves as inputs to computation." — [Source](https://strategyn.com/quantify-your-customers-unmet-needs)
- **[Verbatim]** Definition of underserved and overserved: "a desired outcome represents an opportunity for innovation when (1) it is underserved — the outcome is important to customers, but not well satisfied given the products and services currently available to them, and also when it is (2) overserved — the outcome is well satisfied, but it is not important to customers. When an outcome is underserved, it is a target for value creation. When an outcome is overserved, it is a target for cost reduction." — [Source](https://strategyn.com/quantify-your-customers-unmet-needs)
- **[Verbatim]** Worked example: "If 81% of surgeons, for example, rate an outcome very or extremely important, yet only 30% percent rate it very or extremely satisfied, that outcome would be considered underserved. In contrast, if only 30% of those surveyed rate an outcome very or extremely important and 81% rate it very or extremely satisfied, that outcome would be considered overserved." — [Source](https://strategyn.com/quantify-your-customers-unmet-needs)
- **[Verbatim]** What is being rated: "A desired outcome statement is a uniquely defined need statement that describes how customers measure success when getting a job done." Strategyn's process page adds that outcomes are "solution-free, measurable, and stable over time" and that "A survey is administered to a statistically valid sample of customers, typically 180 to 3,000 respondents." — [Source](https://strategyn.com/quantify-your-customers-unmet-needs); [Source](https://strategyn.com/outcome-driven-innovation-process/) (undated page, copyright 2025)
- **[Verbatim]** Strategyn's four stated assumptions behind the algorithm include "3. Needs that are of higher importance and lower satisfaction are better targets for value creation than those that are of lesser importance and greater satisfaction." and the claim "We have validated it both in practice (86% success rate) and with PhDs in marketing, statistics, and engineering." — [Source](https://strategyn.com/quantify-your-customers-unmet-needs)
- **[Secondary]** Wikipedia states the floor and the weighting: "Ulwick's formula gives twice as much weight to importance as to satisfaction: importance + max(importance-satisfaction,0), where importance and satisfaction are the proportion of high survey responses." The same article, citing a 2012 Analysys Mason report, says "Customers use a 1-to-10 scale to quantify the importance of each desired outcome", which conflicts with Strategyn's top-two-box description above. — [Source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Outcome-Driven_Innovation) (accessed 2026-11-10)
- **[Verbatim]** Intercom's Des Traynor, applying the algorithm to jobs-to-be-done: "importance + (importance-satisfaction)" and "Note: the bracketed element bottoms out at zero, over-serving a job doesn't reduce its opportunity." — Des Traynor, Intercom, 14 May 2014 — [Source](https://www.intercom.com/blog/where-do-you-suck-where-does-it-matter)
- **[Secondary]** Critique of the double weighting: ProductPlan notes that "standard gap analysis treats customers' importance and satisfaction ratings equally" whereas Ulwick's gives importance twice the weight. — [Source](https://www.productplan.com/glossary/opportunity-scoring)
- **[Secondary]** Thresholds are not consistent across secondary sources: a Productboard prompt template uses "15+: Major opportunity (strongly underserved)" and "12-14: Significant opportunity (meaningfully underserved)"; other guides say scores above 10 are "extreme" opportunities and above 12 a "once-in-a-decade gap". None of these pages attributes the bands to Ulwick's text. — [Source](https://www.productboard.com/product-management-prompts-library/opportunity-scoring-framework/); [Source](https://www.simpliaxis.com/resources/opportunity-scoring)
- **[Secondary]** Dan Olsen's Lean Product Playbook (2015) importance-vs-satisfaction framework is described as having been "independently developed" and "very similar" to Ulwick's matrix; summaries of Olsen disagree on the arithmetic (importance × satisfaction as delivered value, versus importance − satisfaction as the gap). — [Source](https://www.uxmatters.com/mt/archives/2020/05/book-excerpt-the-jobs-to-be-done-playbook.php); [Source](https://productandrew.substack.com/p/product-75)

### Inferences
- ODI measures importance and satisfaction per outcome from a representative survey; it has no frequency-of-mention or distinct-author term. A forum-mined "intensity" is at best a proxy for importance, and a count of complaints about an existing tool is a proxy for low satisfaction. The pipeline's additive-or-multiplicative choice is a design decision ODI answers one way (additive, importance double-weighted, gap floored at zero).
- Because Strategyn's inputs are proportions of a sampled population, the score is already normalised for reach. A forum count is not: distinct-author counts scale with community size, so the pipeline must normalise or log-scale frequency before it resembles an ODI "importance" proportion.
- Ulwick's "overserved" category is a reminder that a complaint about something customers do not rate as important is a cost-reduction signal, not a build signal.

### Gaps
- I could not retrieve Ulwick's own text for the numeric underserved thresholds (commonly cited as >10, >12, >15) or the "percentage rating 4 or 5, × 10" normalisation. The ODI book chapter with "A Critique of the Opportunity Algorithm" (odi-jtbd-book-public.vercel.app) returned DEPLOYMENT_NOT_FOUND, and a Substack article promising "critical flaws" is paywalled. Treat the bands as practitioner folklore until checked against *What Customers Want* (2005) or *Jobs to be Done: Theory to Practice* (2016).

## Key question 2: Rob Fitzpatrick's The Mom Test — what counts as evidence versus noise

### Takeaway
The Mom Test treats only specifics about the past (what they did, what they already spend, what they already tried, what the problem costs them) and commitments that cost the speaker something (time, reputation, money) as data; compliments, generic claims, future-tense promises, hypotheticals and "I would pay" are classed as bad data.

### Cited Findings
- **[Verbatim]** The three rules: "1. Talk about their life instead of your idea 2. Ask about specifics in the past instead of generics or opinions about the future 3. Talk less and listen more". — Rob Fitzpatrick, *The Mom Test* (2013), text from a PDF copy hosted by McMaster's The Forge — [Source](https://theforge.mcmaster.ca/wp-content/uploads/2024/02/The-Mom-Test.pdf)
- **[Verbatim]** "Rule of thumb: Anything involving the future is an over-optimistic lie." — [Source](https://theforge.mcmaster.ca/wp-content/uploads/2024/02/The-Mom-Test.pdf)
- **[Verbatim]** "Rule of thumb: If they haven't looked for ways of solving it already, they're not going to look for (or buy) yours." — [Source](https://theforge.mcmaster.ca/wp-content/uploads/2024/02/The-Mom-Test.pdf)
- **[Verbatim]** On "How much would you pay for X?": "Bad question. This is exactly as bad as the last one, except it's more likely to trick you because the number makes it feel rigorous and truthy. How to fix it: Just like the others, fix it by asking about their life as it already is. How much does the problem cost them? How much do they currently pay to solve it? How big is the budget they've allocated?" — [Source](https://theforge.mcmaster.ca/wp-content/uploads/2024/02/The-Mom-Test.pdf)
- **[Verbatim]** On "Would you pay X for a product which did Y?": "Bad question. The fact that you've added a number doesn't help. This is bad for the same reasons as the others: people are overly optimistic about what they would do and want to make you happy. Plus, it's about your idea instead of their life." — [Source](https://theforge.mcmaster.ca/wp-content/uploads/2024/02/The-Mom-Test.pdf)
- **[Verbatim]** The three kinds of bad data: "There are three types of bad data: 1. Compliments 2. Fluff (generics, hypotheticals, and the future) 3. Ideas". And: "Rule of thumb: Compliments are the fool's gold of customer learning: shiny, distracting, and worthless." — [Source](https://theforge.mcmaster.ca/wp-content/uploads/2024/02/The-Mom-Test.pdf)
- **[Verbatim]** "Fluff comes in 3 cuddly shapes: • Generic claims ("I usually", "I always", "I never") • Future-tense promises ("I would", "I will") • Hypothetical maybes ("I might", "I could") When someone starts talking about what they "always" or "usually" or "never" or "would" do, they are giving you generic and hypothetical fluff. Ask good questions that obey The Mom Test to anchor them back to specifics in the past." — [Source](https://theforge.mcmaster.ca/wp-content/uploads/2024/02/The-Mom-Test.pdf)
- **[Verbatim]** The question that separates paid pain from tolerable pain: ""What are the implications of that?" Good question. This distinguishes between I-will-pay-to-solve-that problems and thats-kind-of-annoying-but-I-can-deal-with-it "problems". Some problems have big, costly implications. Others exist but don't actually matter. It behooves you to find out which is which. It also gives you a good pricing signal." — [Source](https://theforge.mcmaster.ca/wp-content/uploads/2024/02/The-Mom-Test.pdf)
- **[Verbatim]** The good-question list includes "Talk me through the last time that happened.", "Talk me through your workflow.", "What else have you tried?", "How are you dealing with it now?", "Where does the money come from?", "Who else should I talk to?" — [Source](https://theforge.mcmaster.ca/wp-content/uploads/2024/02/The-Mom-Test.pdf)
- **[Verbatim]** Commitment and advancement: "Commitment can be cash, but doesn't have to be. Think of it in terms of currency—what are they giving up for you? A compliment costs them nothing, so it's worth nothing and carries no data. The major currencies are time, reputation risk, and cash." Time commitments listed: "Clear next meeting with known goals", "Sitting down to give feedback on wireframes", "Using a trial of the product for a non-trivial period"; reputation: "Intro to peers or team", "Intro to a decision maker (boss, spouse, lawyer)", "Giving a public testimonial or case study"; financial: letter of intent, pre-order, deposit. "The more they give up, the more we can trust them." — [Source](https://theforge.mcmaster.ca/wp-content/uploads/2024/02/The-Mom-Test.pdf)
- **[Verbatim]** On emotion as a signal to dig, not as proof: "any strong emotion is worth exploring. Is someone angry? Dig. Embarrassed? Dig. Overjoyed? Dig!" — [Source](https://theforge.mcmaster.ca/wp-content/uploads/2024/02/The-Mom-Test.pdf)
- **[Verbatim]** On segment coherence: "Rule of thumb: Start broad and don't zoom in until you've found a strong signal, both with your whole business and with every conversation." — [Source](https://theforge.mcmaster.ca/wp-content/uploads/2024/02/The-Mom-Test.pdf); **[Secondary]** reader notes add the book's rule "If you aren't finding consistent problems and goals, you probably don't have a specific enough customer segment." — [Source](https://www.greaterwrong.com/posts/fuktKYSvzFMTfPpeT/the-mom-test-summary-and-thoughts)
- **[Secondary]** A reader applying the book reports the exact failure mode the pipeline must guard against: "People claiming that something is a big problem, that they'd pay money for a solution, even getting emotional about it. And nevertheless, they don't actually give a shit." — [Source](https://www.greaterwrong.com/posts/fuktKYSvzFMTfPpeT/the-mom-test-summary-and-thoughts)
- **[Secondary]** Michael Lynch's book report lists the rules and the data hierarchy in the same terms (past behaviour, "how much problem X currently costs them", commitment types). — [Source](https://mtlynch.io/book-reports/the-mom-test/)
- **[Verbatim]** The official site describes the book's purpose as "How to figure out whether someone is really going to buy?" and "how to ask questions that provide real data"; it links no free excerpt. — [Source](https://www.momtestbook.com/)

### Inferences
- For a text-mining pipeline, the Mom Test implies a strict evidence hierarchy: (1) reports of money already spent or hired help, (2) described workarounds and tools already tried, (3) specific past incidents with a cost or time figure, (4) emotional intensity (a prompt to dig, not proof), and at the bottom (5) hypotheticals and "I would pay" statements. A "willingness-to-pay keyword" hit on future-tense phrasing ("I'd pay for", "would love to pay") is, by the book's rule, the lowest grade of evidence, while "I pay $X/month for", "we hired", "I built a spreadsheet to" is the highest.
- The book's "If they haven't looked for ways of solving it already" rule means a complaint with no mention of any attempted fix should be discounted relative to one that names tools tried.
- Commitment currencies (time, reputation, cash) have forum analogues: a long detailed post, a user publicly recommending a paid tool, an author returning to the thread, or a bounty/"willing to pay for a fix" post are weak proxies for commitment; passive upvotes are compliments.

### Gaps
- The verbatim quotes come from a PDF copy of the book hosted on a university site, not from the publisher. Page numbering in that copy differs from print editions. No freely available excerpt from Fitzpatrick's own sites was found.

## Key question 3: YC and Paul Graham — organic ideas, schlep blindness, live in the future, well vs crater, tarpit ideas, YC checklists

### Takeaway
Graham's test is depth of need in a small group ("a small number of people want a large amount"), noticed organically rather than thought up; YC's partners operationalise this as problem attributes (popular, growing, urgent, expensive, mandatory, frequent), interview questions (cost today, frequency, budget), and warnings about tarpit ideas, which are widely felt, repeatedly attempted, structurally blocked problems that draw positive feedback.

### Cited Findings
Paul Graham
- **[Verbatim]** Well vs crater: "you can either build something a large number of people want a small amount, or something a small number of people want a large amount. Choose the latter. Not all ideas of that type are good startup ideas, but nearly all good startup ideas are of that type." and "Imagine a graph whose x axis represents all the people who might want what you're making and whose y axis represents how much they want it. If you invert the scale on the y axis, you can envision companies as holes. Google is an immense crater ... You can either dig a hole that's broad but shallow, or one that's narrow and deep, like a well. Made-up startup ideas are usually of the first type." — Paul Graham, "How to Get Startup Ideas", November 2012 — [Source](https://paulgraham.com/startupideas.html)
- **[Verbatim]** The urgency test: "When you have an idea for a startup, ask yourself: who wants this right now? Who wants this so much that they'll use it even when it's a crappy version one made by a two-person startup they've never heard of? If you can't answer that, the idea is probably bad." — [Source](https://paulgraham.com/startupideas.html)
- **[Verbatim]** Depth, not narrowness, is the requirement, and the well is necessary but not sufficient: "It's depth you need; you get narrowness as a byproduct of optimizing for depth (and speed)." and "while demand shaped like a well is almost a necessary condition for a good startup idea, it's not a sufficient one ... - **[Verbatim]** Sitcom ideas and why friends' reactions mislead: "At YC we call these "made-up" or "sitcom" startup ideas." ... - **[Verbatim]** "Live in the future, then build what's missing." and "The verb you want to be using with respect to startup ideas is not "think up" but "notice." - **[Verbatim]** Pay test for unsexy ideas: "Make something unsexy that people will pay you for." and "A good trick for bypassing the schlep and to some extent the unsexy filter is to ask what you wish someone else would build, so that you could use it. - **[Verbatim]** Schlep blindness: "It means a tedious, unpleasant task." ... "Your unconscious won't even let you see ideas that involve painful schleps. That's schlep blindness." ... ### Inferences
- Graham's well is a two-dimensional object (how many × how badly) and he says explicitly that depth dominates. A pipeline that adds frequency and intensity will systematically over-rank broad, shallow "sitcom" complaints; weighting intensity above distinct-author count is closer to his rule. "Who wants this so much that they'll use a crappy version one" is a question about the top of the intensity distribution, not its mean.
- YC's three numeric interview facts (cost today, frequency of encounter, budget/authority) map directly onto the pipeline's intensity, recurrence and willingness-to-pay axes, and Migicovsky's assembly-line example is the reachability/authority failure: a frequent sufferer without budget is not a buyer.
- Tarpit screening is a separate, negative check the pipeline does not have: high frequency of complaint + many prior attempts + no survivor is a red flag, not a green one. A "has this been tried many times and failed" lookup (Friedman's "First, Google it") belongs next to the software-solvability multiplier, because tarpits are exactly the problems where solvability looks high and is not.
- On competition, Graham and Friedman are consistent: existing paid competitors validate demand; the disagreement they anticipate (saturation) is answered with "only if the incumbent has lock-in" (Graham) and "only if you have no insight they all missed" (Friedman). Rob Walling's 5 P.M. framework (below) records competition as a neutral question ("How much? Big companies or other startups?") rather than a positive.

## Key question 4: Sean Ellis's 40% very-disappointed test and Rahul Vohra's Superhuman PMF engine

### Takeaway
Both are post-launch measures applied to active users ("used the product at least twice in the last two weeks"); the 40% threshold came from comparing nearly 100 startups and Ellis calls it "a bit arbitrary". ### Inferences
- The Ellis question measures dependence on an existing product. Complaints about a tool people say they would happily drop are "not disappointed" signals for that category.
- Vohra's segmentation step suggests scoring per persona rather than pooling: identify the sub-group whose complaints are most intense and most specific, profile it, and weight distinct authors inside that segment more than authors outside it. - The 40% threshold, 40-respondent floor and "twice in the last two weeks" filter cannot be transferred to forum data; what transfers is the discipline of a fixed, repeated question and a cohort filter on engaged users (for forums: authors with repeat posts in the niche, not drive-by posters).

## Key question 6: Published scoring rubrics (RICE, ICE, Kano, opportunity scoring, pain × frequency × WTP) and how practitioners combine frequency with intensity

### Takeaway
Product rubrics split into additive (Ulwick's opportunity score; ICE as an average) and multiplicative (RICE, Cohen's problem chain, vendor "frequency × intensity × solvability" formulas). The practitioners who justify multiplication do so because the factors are logical ANDs (Cohen) or because effort is a divisor of total impact (Intercom); no primary source found argues for log scaling, and distinct-voice counting appears only implicitly (Reach in RICE counts people per period; Cohen counts named buyers).

### Inferences
- Across the sources, the factors that practitioners multiply are gating conditions (budget, timing, differentiation, effort); the factors they add or band are degrees of the same thing (importance vs satisfaction). By that logic the pipeline's reachability, willingness-to-pay and software-solvability are AND-type gates that belong as multipliers (with a floor above zero to avoid annihilating a signal on one noisy keyword), while intensity, frequency, recurrence and persistence are degree measures better combined additively or as a weighted sum, with intensity weighted above count (Graham).
- Cohen's power-of-ten scales are the only explicit precedent for log-scaling counts; applying log to distinct-author counts before combining with intensity is consistent with his approach and with Ulwick's use of proportions rather than raw counts.
- RICE's "Reach ... per time period" and Vohra's "number of requests" both count people, not messages; a distinct-author count per time window is the forum equivalent, and ProdPad's warning means that count should be read as "which problem", not "which feature".

### Gaps
- No primary source was found that compares additive versus multiplicative combination of frequency and intensity for complaint mining specifically; the comparison above is my inference from the rubrics' structure. ICE's original formula (average vs product) could not be settled from Ellis's own text.

## Key question 7: Known failure modes — vocal minority, say vs do, free things, social gripes, vitamins, no-budget markets

### Takeaway
Every framework surveyed names at least one of the pipeline-relevant traps: feedback portals over-represent power users and extreme opinions (ProdPad); stated intent is "an over-optimistic lie" (Fitzpatrick); communities that recommend only free tools resist paid products (Kahl); tarpit problems are widely felt but structurally unsolvable (YC); vitamins are optional by definition (Walling, Kahl); and whole markets lack budget regardless of pain (Cohen).

### Cited Findings
- **[Verbatim]** Vocal minority: "The customers most likely to visit a feedback portal regularly are power users" and "the people most motivated to participate hold extreme opinions, either very positive or very negative." "The moderately satisfied majority tends to stay quiet." Position bias: "This is a classic position bias effect, and it compounds over time." — ProdPad, April 16, 2026 — [Source](https://www.prodpad.com/blog/feedback-voting-bias-in-product-decisions/); **[Secondary]** a survey vendor claims brands hear from "less than 1% of their customer base, which we call the "vocal minority."" — [Source](https://www.alchemer.com/resources/blog/most-brands-hear-from-less-than-1-percent-customers/)
- **[Verbatim]** Say vs do: "Rule of thumb: Anything involving the future is an over-optimistic lie." and "people are overly optimistic about what they would do and want to make you happy." — [Source](https://theforge.mcmaster.ca/wp-content/uploads/2024/02/The-Mom-Test.pdf); Graham's version: friends "say "Yeah, maybe I could see using something like that." ... Sum that reaction across the entire population, and you have zero users." — [Source](https://paulgraham.com/startupideas.html); Jackson's version: enthusiasm at a demo, five copies sold. — [Source](https://justinjackson.ca/ultimate-validation)
- **[Verbatim]** Complaints about free things / free-tool communities: "If every single recommendation is a free tool or resource, you might run into trouble charging for something comparable later." — [Source](https://thebootstrappedfounder.com/the-shape-of-a-problem-in-the-wild/); Graham: "It's hard to compete directly with open source software ... there has to be some part you can charge for." — [Source](https://paulgraham.com/organic.html); Cohen on consumers complaining about $15/year. — [Source](https://longform.asmartbear.com/problem/)

- **[Verbatim]** No-budget markets: "college kids have no money ... - **[Verbatim]** Not-self-aware or not-a-priority markets: "though everyone agrees they have the "problem" of a non-accessible website or product, most don't have the will to act." — [Source](https://longform.asmartbear.com/problem/)
- **[Verbatim]** Audience is not demand: "Jason Cohen's blog had 40,000 subscribers. - **[Verbatim]** Point-in-time pain looks intense but does not recur: "Websites for events and occasions are an example, as are tools that solve temporary problems like sophisticated code profilers or load-testing tools ... people didn't proactively need the product ... but then suddenly did ... and then didn't any more". — [Source](https://longform.asmartbear.com/problem/)
- **[Verbatim]** Novice skew in help requests: "beginners will ask for help faster and more often, which will skew the quantitative distribution of "asking for help" messages towards the novices in any given field." — [Source](https://thebootstrappedfounder.com/the-shape-of-a-problem-in-the-wild/)

### Inferences
- Each failure mode has a concrete pipeline check: (1) vocal minority: cap or log distinct-author counts and weight by author expertise/role; (2) say vs do: grade willingness-to-pay keywords by tense and specificity, with past-tense spend and named prices above conditional phrasing; (3) free things: detect whether the thread's recommended alternatives are free or paid and whether the incumbent being complained about is free; (4) tarpits: a "prior attempts" lookup that lowers the solvability multiplier when many dead products exist; (5) vitamins: require a cost, time-lost or consequence statement (Fitzpatrick's "implications") for a high intensity score; (6) no budget: classify the community as consumer, aspirational, B2B or enterprise (Walling's B2C/B2A/B2B/B2E) and treat budget authority as a gate; (7) point-in-time pain: separate recurrence (same author, repeated) from persistence (many authors over time) so that seasonal spikes are not scored as recurring.
- Cohen's "Self-Aware" factor is structurally inflated in forum data (posters are the self-aware minority), so a forum-derived score estimates the top of the awareness distribution, not the market.

### Gaps
- The vocal-minority figures come from vendors of feedback software (Alchemer, ProdPad); no peer-reviewed study of complaint-forum representativeness was found in this pass. The HBR "1% write 40% of reviews" statistic surfaced only on low-quality aggregators and is not cited here.
